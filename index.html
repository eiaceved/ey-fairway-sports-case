01

How an EY-Parthenon-style growth case works

Growth cases ask whether a company should move into something new: a market, a product, or a business model. In an EY-Parthenon-style case you lead: size the market, test the economics, and make the call.

Put simply, they want structured thinking, comfortable market sizing, and a clear decision. "Go, but start small, here" beats "it depends." Formats vary by office, so confirm the details with your recruiter.

How the interview runs

Based on public prep guides

  • Usually an online numbers test first, sometimes with a recorded video interview.
  • First round: one or two interviews of 45 to 60 minutes, each mixing fit questions with a candidate-led case.
  • Final round: two or three interviews with senior leaders. Some offices add a group case or a written case you present in a few slides.

What makes a growth case different

Four habits

  • Size the market before anything else.
  • Check who else is moving in: a hot market can get crowded fast.
  • Prove the economics of one unit before rolling out fifty.
  • Ask why this company should win, not just whether the market is attractive.

What the case feels like

  • Market. How many customers, and how fast is it growing?
  • Competition. How many rivals, and what's happening to their prices and how full they are?
  • Economics. What does one location earn, and how long until it pays back?
  • Right to win. What does this company have that rivals don't?
  • Decision. Go, no-go, or start small, and exactly how.

A useful order for any market entry case

  1. MarketBig and growing?
  2. CompetitionGetting crowded?
  3. EconomicsDoes one unit work?
  4. Right to winWhy us?

Then decide how to enter: build, partner, or buy

Ten terms to drop naturally

Market entry

Moving into a business the company isn't in today. The core question: can we make money there, and why us?

Supply and demand

When supply, like new clubs, grows faster than demand, like new players, prices and utilization fall.

Utilization

The share of capacity that's actually used, like the share of court-hours that get booked.

Seasonality

Demand that rises and falls with the time of year. Indoor courts are busiest in winter.

Unit economics

The profit of one unit, like one club, before you multiply it across many.

Incremental cost

Only the costs that change because of a decision. Rent you pay either way doesn't count.

Break-even

The point where revenue exactly covers costs. Here, the utilization a club needs to stop losing money.

Payback period

How long profits take to repay the upfront cost. 1.2 million earning 360 thousand a year pays back in about 3 years.

Right to win

An advantage that lets a company beat rivals in a new market, like cheaper space or existing customers.

Pilot

A small test before a full rollout, with a clear target that decides whether to expand.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client is Fairway Sports, a national sporting goods retailer with about 300 big-box stores and about 4 billion dollars in revenue.

As more shopping has moved online, many Fairway stores now have large areas of unused space, and Fairway pays rent on that space either way. Meanwhile, pickleball has grown quickly, and indoor pickleball clubs are opening in many cities.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • Is the goal profit from the clubs, or more shoppers in stores?
    Mostly a new profit stream. More shoppers in the store would be a bonus.
  • How much space, and in how many stores?
    About 120 stores have roughly 20,000 square feet free, enough for about 8 courts each.
  • Does Fairway pay extra for that space?
    No. It already pays rent on it under long leases, whether the space is used or not.
  • Any budget or timeline?
    Up to 50 million dollars over three years. The board wants a plan in two months.
  • Does Fairway have any link to pickleball today?
    It sells pickleball gear, which has been one of its fastest-growing categories.

The third question is the one that cracks this case: if the rent is already paid, it isn't a cost of the clubs.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Okay. How do you want to approach this?

Your move

Take 60 seconds to sketch your structure. Then walk through it, say where you'd start, and ask for specific data.

Strong framework

Should Fairway turn unused store space into pickleball clubs, and how?
1Market
  • Players near Fairway stores
  • Growth, and how long it lasts
  • Indoor demand by season
2Competition
  • How fast clubs are opening
  • Rivals' prices and utilization
  • Free outdoor courts
3Club economics
  • Courts × hours × use × price
  • Incremental costs only
  • Break-even and payback
4Right to win and how
  • Free space, store traffic
  • Gear sales and brand
  • Build, partner, or buy

Say it like this

"I'd look at four things. First, the market: how many people play near Fairway stores, how fast that's growing, and how much of it is indoors. Second, competition: how many clubs are opening, and what's happening to their prices and utilization. Third, the economics of one club, counting only the costs that change because of this decision. Fourth, why Fairway should win, and whether to build, partner, or buy. My hypothesis is that Fairway has a real cost advantage because the space is already paid for, but that the market could be getting crowded. I'd like to start with market and competitor trends."

Why it works

  • It sizes demand and supply together, which catches a crowding market early.
  • It flags incremental cost up front, which is the key insight in this case.
  • It ends with "how," because the CEO asked that too.

If you blank, "market, competition, economics, right to win" works for almost any market entry case.

05

Exhibit 1: the market and the competition

Interviewer

Here's what you asked for: six years of data for the metro areas where Fairway has unused space. What stands out?

Which finding should you lead with?

Players grew from 300 thousand to 960 thousand, but growth slowed from about 40 percent a year to about 7. Clubs went from 10 to 80, eight times as many. With supply outrunning demand, rival utilization fell from 70 to 45 percent, and prices fell from 60 to 45 dollars a court-hour. Those are classic signs of a crowding market. One bright spot: half of players now play indoors in winter.

Then

Say the insight, tie it to the decision, and ask for your next piece of data.

Say it like this

"Pickleball is still growing, but it's slowing, from about 40 percent a year to 7. Meanwhile clubs have multiplied eight times, so rival utilization dropped to 45 percent and prices fell by a quarter. That's a crowding market, so any club Fairway opens has to work at today's lower prices and utilization. Can I see what a club costs to run, and how Fairway's costs would compare with a rival's?"

Notice the pattern: insight, then so-what for the decision, then your own next ask. That's how you move a candidate-led case forward.

06

Exhibit 2: a rival club vs. a Fairway club

Interviewer

Good. Here are yearly running costs for a typical 8-court rival club, next to what a Fairway club would cost. A typical rival brings in about 810 thousand dollars a year at today's prices. What's your read?

What's the so-what?

A rival brings in about 810 thousand but spends 850, so it loses about 40 thousand a year. Fairway would spend about 450, because the space is already paid for. Counting that rent would be a mistake, since Fairway pays it whether or not it builds courts. That's the incremental cost idea. So at the same prices, a Fairway club would earn about 360 thousand. If the market shakes out, rivals close first.

Then

Synthesize both exhibits in three sentences.

Say it like this

"The market is crowding: player growth has slowed while clubs have multiplied, so prices and utilization are falling. At today's levels a typical rival loses money, but Fairway, paying no extra rent, would still earn about 360 thousand per club. So Fairway has a real cost edge, but it should enter carefully, in metros where clubs are still scarce."

Your move

Say the formula first: revenue equals courts times hours times utilization times price. Then profit, break-even utilization, payback, and the network.

  1. 1 · Capacity8 courts × 5,000 hours40,000

  3. 3 · Profit$810K − $450K. A rival: $810K − $850K = −$40K$360K
  4. 4 · Break-even utilization$450K ÷ $1.8M full capacity. Rival: $850K ÷ $1.8M ≈ 47%25%
  5. 5 · Payback$1.2M ÷ $360K≈3.3 yrs

Quick check: at what utilization does a Fairway club break even?

Running costs of 450 thousand divided by full-capacity revenue of 1.8 million is 25 percent. Everyday version: if a lemonade stand costs 25 dollars a day to run and could take in 100 dollars if every cup sold, it needs to sell a quarter of its cups to break even.

Revenue = $810K a club Profit = $360K, payback ≈ 3.3 years Break-even = 25% vs. rivals' 47%
Then

Summarize the math in two sentences, and add one sanity check.

Say it like this

"One 8-court club brings in about 810 thousand at today's prices and utilization and costs 450 thousand to run, so it earns about 360 thousand and pays back in a little over 3 years. It breaks even at 25 percent utilization, while a rival paying rent needs about 47, which is above today's 45. Forty clubs in metros with few rivals would earn about 14 million a year for 48 million, within budget."

If the interviewer asks, "What if utilization falls to 30 percent in a shakeout?": "Revenue drops to about 540 thousand, so profit falls to about 90 thousand, still positive. Most rivals would be losing a lot of money by then, which is exactly why Fairway's cost edge matters."

08

Score the ways to play

Interviewer

Say Fairway goes ahead. Here are six ways it could play. Score each for ease and impact, from 1 to 5. Which three would you combine?

Your move

Score each option out loud with a one-line reason, then name the three you'd combine.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd combine three. Run leagues and coaching to fill quiet hours, because utilization makes or breaks a club. Build clubs in unused space, but only in metros with few rivals. And bundle memberships with gear discounts to bring players into the store. Leasing the space to an operator is easier, but it hands away Fairway's cost advantage."

    Which three would you combine?

    B, A, and C score highest and reinforce each other: build where clubs are scarce, fill the courts, and turn players into shoppers. D is easy but gives away the cost edge. E, buying a chain, is costly in a market that may be about to shake out; it may be cheaper to buy later. F only works in summer, when indoor demand is lowest anyway.

    09

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The CEO just walked in. You have 60 seconds. Should Fairway do this, and how?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

    Strong answer

    Answer3 reasonsRiskNext steps

    "Fairway should enter, but start small: open 5 pilot clubs in unused store space in metros with few rival clubs, then expand to about 40 if they hit their targets."

    "First, Fairway has a real cost edge. Because the space is already paid for, a club breaks even at about 25 percent utilization, while rivals need about 47. Each club earns about 360 thousand dollars a year and pays back in about 3 years."

    "Second, the market is crowding, so where Fairway opens matters. Clubs have multiplied eight times while player growth slowed to about 7 percent, and rival utilization is down to 45 percent. Fairway should only open where clubs are still scarce."

    "Third, Fairway can fill courts better than rivals. Leagues and coaching fill quiet hours, and bundling memberships with gear discounts brings players into the store, where pickleball gear is already a 130 million dollar category."

    "The main risk is that pickleball's growth stalls and the shakeout is worse than expected. The pilot limits that: if the first 5 clubs don't average about 40 percent utilization within a year, Fairway stops at 5. Next steps: pick 5 stores in cold-weather metros with few rivals, design leagues and coaching before opening, and set up the gear bundle."

    The rollout plan

    Months 1–6Pilot
    • Build 5 clubs in the best-fit stores.
    • Launch leagues and coaching from day one.
    • Set up the gear bundle.
    Months 7–12Prove
    • Track utilization by month.
    • Gate: about 40% average utilization.
    Year 2Scale
    • Roll out to about 40 metros.
    • Watch for weak rivals to buy cheaply in a shakeout.

    Indicators to track

    • Average utilization. Target: 40 percent or more.
    • Profit per club. Target: 300 thousand dollars a year or more.
    • Members who also buy gear in the store. Target: half or more.
    • Payback per club. Target: 4 years or less.

    Bonus drills: market sizing and growth

    Two short reps. Market sizing is the most common add-on question in growth cases.

    Market sizing: clubs one metro can support

    Interviewer

    How many 8-court indoor pickleball clubs can a metro area of 2 million people support in winter?

    Your move

    Work from demand to supply: people, players, court-hours they want, then divide by what one club can handle. Say each assumption out loud.

    1. Players2M people × 5%100K
    2. × 50% play indoors in winterIndoor players50K
    3. Court-hours a week50K players, once a week, 4 per court12,500
    4. Over a 20-week winter12,500 × 20250K
    5. Clubs supported8 × 14 hours × 140 days ≈ 15,000. 250K ÷ 15K≈17
    6. So what10 clubs already: room for more. 20 or more: overbuilt, skip it✓

    All inputs are interview-style assumptions, not market research.

    Market growth: is pickleball a fad?

    Interviewer

    Is pickleball a lasting business, or a fad? How would that change your recommendation?

    Your move

    Split growth into more players and more spend per player, then list drivers and headwinds.

    Strong answer

    Market growth = more players × more spend per player
    Easy to start

    Simple to learn, cheap gear, and fun at almost any age.

    Social

    Games of four and leagues make it a regular habit with friends.

    Weather

    In cold or rainy places, indoor courts are the only option for months.

    More spend per player

    Leagues, coaching, and better gear raise what each player spends.

    Headwinds

    • Player growth is slowing fast.
    • Free public courts compete with paid clubs, especially in summer.
    • Players could move on to the next racket sport, like padel.

    Quick math

    At 40 percent a year, players double in under 2 years. At 7 percent, it takes about 10 years: 72 divided by 7. That slowdown is why Fairway should pilot first.

    "I think pickleball lasts as a sport, but the boom phase is over, and the club market will shake out. That supports entering with a cost advantage and a small pilot, not a big bet on continued fast growth."
